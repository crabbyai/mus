PhD research

Learning by doing: the idea Canada needs now

Dr. Rahman's doctoral thesis at McMaster studied learning by doing, one of the most important ideas in growth economics. It explains why the countries that start building get cheaper, faster and better, and why the ones that wait fall behind.

The idea

The more you make something, the better you get at it. Every unit produced teaches workers, managers and engineers something new. Costs fall, quality rises and productivity grows, without anyone inventing a new machine.

1936Theodore Wright found that each time aircraft output doubled, the labour needed per plane fell by a steady percentage. This became known as Wright's law.1962Kenneth Arrow, later a Nobel laureate, formalised it in "The Economic Implications of Learning by Doing", making experience itself a source of economic growth.WWIIU.S. Liberty ship yards cut build times dramatically as they built more ships. It became the classic case study.TodaySolar panels, batteries and wind turbines follow the same curve. That's why their costs have fallen so far.

Why it matters for Canada

Productivity. Canada's productivity gap with the U.S. is one of the country's biggest economic problems. Learning by doing explains where productivity comes from, and how policy can speed it up.

Industrial strategy. Battery plants, critical minerals processing, shipbuilding and clean energy: whoever produces first gets down the cost curve first. Canada can't afford to only export raw resources and import the finished goods.

Tariffs. When U.S. tariffs shrink Canadian output, firms lose more than sales. They also lose the experience that would have made them more competitive. Policy has to protect that learning.

People. Apprenticeships, on-the-job training and newcomers' experience are learning by doing in the workforce. Every year that skills sit unused is lost productivity.
